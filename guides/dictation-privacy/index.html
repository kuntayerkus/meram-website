Seven questions to ask
a dictation app.

Dictation software hears everything you say while it is open and can type into any window you have. That is a lot of trust for a category that mostly markets itself on speed.

Updated August 20268 minute read

Every question below has a version Meram fails. They are here in the order that matters, with our own answers written next to them, because a checklist that only flatters the product publishing it is an advertisement.

1. Where does the audio go?

There are three honest answers, and marketing pages routinely blur them: on-device (the audio never leaves), hosted (it goes to the vendor’s servers), and bring your own key (it goes straight to a provider you have an account with).

Watch for “private by design” or “your data is yours” used where one of those three words belongs. Those phrases are compatible with sending every word you say to a third party.

Meram: bring your own key. Audio goes to Groq or OpenAI with your key, on your account. There is no Meram server for it to reach.

2. Is it retained, and for how long?

Sending is one question; keeping is another. Ask whether audio is written to disk at all, whether transcripts are stored server-side, and what the retention window is. If the answer is a link to a provider’s policy rather than the vendor’s own, at least you know who to read.

Meram: recordings are not kept unless you switch on “keep audio”. Transcripts live in a local database. Retention at the provider is between you and them, and that is a real dependency rather than a technicality.

3. Is there really an offline mode?

“Offline” is the most abused word in this category. It sometimes means “the app opens without a connection”. Ask directly: can it transcribe with the network switched off? Then switch the network off and try.

Meram: no. On-device Whisper is written and deliberately disabled until it can be packaged with pinned checksums and a verification corpus. Windows’ built-in voice typing is better than Meram on this specific axis today.

4. What does it read from your screen?

Good dictation wants context — which app you are in, sometimes the text around your cursor. That is genuinely useful and genuinely invasive, so it should be a setting you turned on, not a default you discovered.

Meram: the focused application’s name is used to match tone. Reading the field around your cursor is best-effort and limited. Screen grounding is a separate setting and is off by default.

5. How is your API key stored?

If you bring your own key, that key is money. Ask whether it goes to the operating system’s keystore, whether there is a plaintext fallback, and whether any copy of it is written anywhere else.

Meram: the OS keystore where available, with a plaintext compatibility fallback where it is not — and a second copy in a settings backup file, with the same protection as the original. Both facts are on the privacy page, because the second one is exactly the sort of detail that usually is not.

6. What happens when it can’t type?

Rarely asked, and it is the one that bites. If the window changed while you were talking, does the text land in the wrong application? Does it silently vanish? Does it get pasted into a password field?

Meram: the target is verified against the window handle, the process, that process’s start time and the focused control before a key is sent. Unproven, and it goes to your clipboard. A password field, an elevated window or Secure Input, and nothing is written at all — not even to the clipboard.

7. Is there always-on listening?

A wakeword is a permanently open microphone. That may be a trade you want, but you should be the one making it, and the interface should show you while the stream is open.

Meram: wakeword is off by default. When on, it holds a second, permanently live microphone stream, and the capsule shows a microphone badge for the whole time it is open.

The general rule

Trust the vendor that names its limits over the one that only names its strengths. A privacy page listing four things that are not private is worth more than a landing page with a padlock on it — because the first one can be checked, and checking it is the whole point.
